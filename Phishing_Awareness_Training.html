Exhibit B

Recognizing a fake website

A phishing link usually lands you on a page built to look identical to the real one. The differences are almost always in the details, not the design.

Spoofed page
https://accounts-secure-login.support-verify.com/signin
  • Domain has extra words tacked on ("secure-login", "support-verify")
  • No consistent padlock/certificate details on inspection, or a certificate issued days ago
  • Asks for password and extra info (SSN, full card number, security answers) up front
  • Slightly off logo resolution, spacing, or outdated branding
  • Misspelled form labels or broken links elsewhere on the page
Legitimate page
https://accounts.realcompany.com/signin
  • Domain is short and matches the company's actual root domain exactly
  • Only asks for what's normally required to sign in
  • Certificate belongs to the company, established well before today
  • Consistent branding, working footer links, real support contact
  • You reached it by typing the address yourself or a saved bookmark — not a link in an email

Note on padlocks: a padlock icon only means the connection is encrypted — it does not mean the site is trustworthy. Attackers can and do get certificates for fake domains.

Exhibit C

The social-engineering playbook

Phishing works because it exploits predictable human responses, not technical flaws. These are the levers attackers pull most often.

01 · Authority

Impersonating power

Messages posing as a CEO, IT admin, bank, or government agency — people are conditioned to comply quickly with perceived authority.

02 · Urgency & fear

The ticking clock

"Act now or lose access." Deadlines and threatened consequences short-circuit careful thinking.

03 · Pretexting

A believable story

An attacker builds a plausible scenario — a vendor invoice, an HR request, a delivery issue — to justify asking for sensitive info.

04 · Baiting

Something enticing

Free gift cards, a "salary review" attachment, or a USB drive left in the parking lot — curiosity or greed does the rest.

05 · Vishing / smishing

Phone and text phishing

Phishing isn't only email — attackers call or text posing as support staff, delivery services, or coworkers to extract information verbally.

06 · Quid pro quo

A favor for a favor

"I'll fix your slow laptop, just install this tool first" — offering help in exchange for access or credentials.

Exhibit D

Case files: real incidents

Publicly reported examples of how these tactics have played out at scale.

2016

A single spoofed "reset your password" email

A high-profile political campaign staffer received an email disguised as a Google security alert asking him to reset his password. Clicking through handed his real credentials to attackers, exposing years of email correspondence. The message used a shortened link and urgent security-alert framing — no malware required, just a convincing prompt.

Closed
2020

Phone-based social engineering at scale

Attackers called employees at a major social media company posing as internal IT staff to get help-desk credentials, then used that access to take over a number of high-profile verified accounts. No phishing email at all — just a convincing phone pretext and inside knowledge of internal tools.

Closed
2015

Fraudulent wire transfer via lookalike domain

A networking hardware company lost tens of millions of dollars after employees were tricked by emails impersonating executives and outside law firms, requesting confidential wire transfers through a business email compromise scheme built on lookalike domains.

Closed
Exhibit E

Best practices — the field checklist

Click each box as a self-check. Nothing here is saved — it's just to help the habits stick.

Check the sender's actual address

Not just the display name — the full domain after the @.

Hover before you click

Preview the real destination URL of any link before clicking it.

Slow down on urgency

Treat any "act immediately or else" message as a reason to pause, not rush.

Verify money or credential requests out-of-band

Call the person or team using a known number — never one provided in the message itself.

Never enter credentials from an email link

Navigate to the site directly by typing the address or using a saved bookmark instead.

Use multi-factor authentication everywhere it's offered

It won't stop every attack, but it stops most stolen-password attempts cold.

Report, don't just delete

Use your organization's "report phishing" button so security teams can warn others and block the sender.

Exhibit F

Field test

Six quick questions. You'll get feedback after each answer.

Question 1 of 6
An email from "Amazon" has the sender address delivery@amaz0n-support.com. What's the first thing this tells you?
Correct instinct: check the domain, not the display name. "amaz0n-support.com" is not Amazon's real domain — a zero swapped for an "o" is a classic lookalike trick.
Question 2 of 6
A message threatens your account will be deleted in 2 hours unless you click a link immediately. This is an example of:
Right — artificial deadlines are designed to trigger a fast, unverified reaction instead of careful checking.
Question 3 of 6
You get a call from someone claiming to be internal IT, asking for your password to "fix an issue." What should you do?
Legitimate IT staff never need your actual password. Verify independently through a channel you already trust, not one the caller provides.
Question 4 of 6
A website shows a padlock icon in the address bar. What does that guarantee?
Exactly — attackers can get valid certificates for fake domains too. The padlock is about encryption in transit, not the site's identity or intent.
Question 5 of 6
Your "CFO" emails asking for an urgent wire transfer, replying-to a slightly different address than usual. Best move?
Business email compromise scams rely on exactly this scenario. Always verify unusual payment requests through a separate, known channel — replying to the same email doesn't help if that email is spoofed.
Question 6 of 6
You're fairly sure an email is a phishing attempt. What's the best next step?
Reporting lets your security team block the sender and warn coworkers who may have received the same message — deleting alone only protects you.
